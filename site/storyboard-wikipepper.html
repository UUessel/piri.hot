Frame 4Which pepper is this? The pepper search

In the app today

For the pepper in your hand whose name you do not know. Lookalikes such as habanero and Scotch bonnet show together; the user picks and lands on the pepper page. It works on the pod colour and shape stored for every pepper, which in part 2 come from WikiPepper.

Part 2How WikiPepper fits in

WikiPepper becomes the one source of the catalog, the app says so on every screen, and it sends people to WikiPepper.

Frame 6One source of facts, one layer on top

Proposal

What comes from WikiPepper and what Mouth on Fire adds. Nothing on the right is a second source of facts: Mouth on Fire is responsible for it, around WikiPepper's data.

From WikiPepper, and only from there

  • The list of peppers itself: WikiPepper's 5,300 instead of the 315 I have now. The WikiPepper id is the key of every pepper.
  • The English name, the species, the origin.
  • The heat range in Scoville. Where my current values differ, WikiPepper's win and the difference goes back to WikiPepper.
  • Pod colour and shape, for "Which pepper is this?".
  • The photo, with the photographer's name and licence, and WikiPepper's own mark stamped in.

Added by Mouth on Fire

  • The story on each pepper page, in eight languages.
  • The pepper name in 27 languages, translated from WikiPepper's English name.
  • The six heat stage names (Sweet Nothing to Dragon Roar) and the "how many jalapeños" line.
  • The fun fact and the average weight, used for dishes.

Frame 13Updates flow both ways

Proposal

WikiPepper's database stays the single source of truth. An API is a way for one computer system to ask another for data automatically, without a person copying anything. Through the API the Mouth on Fire server fetches WikiPepper's changes every night and the app shows them. Corrections go the other way: a tap on "Tell WikiPepper" reaches the server, I look at it once so WikiPepper gets no noise, and it goes to WikiPepper's curators.
